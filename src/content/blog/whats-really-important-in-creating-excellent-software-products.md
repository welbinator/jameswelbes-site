So, I recently applied for a job and during the application process, they asked me a question that I actually thought was pretty good. It wasn't one of those really annoying 'tell us about a time when you encountered a struggle and how did you work through it?' questions. This was actually a real question that a normal person would want to know about another normal person applying for a job.

The question was: "Briefly describe what you think is important to the process of creating excellent software products."

It's a fun question because it's pretty open ended but not frustratingly vague. You can go in a lot of different directions with it. But it's a real question. It doesn't require you to dig deep into the memory banks and try to recall some random thing that happened at some point in your career.

I don't talk about product management a lot because I don't really fancy myself as being such an expert that anybody cares what I have to say on the topic, but something about this question made me want to talk through this one out loud.

## The Core Tension: New Features vs. Maintenance

In product management there's the concept of adding new features to a product versus maintaining the product. This is one of the challenges of being a Product Manager,you're always trying to balance those two things because you can't do all the things all at once.

Every second your developer spends coding something, they're either adding a new feature to your product, or they're maintaining the product in some way, fixing bugs or refactoring code. They can't do both. So you as a Product Manager have to prioritize this, and come up with the plan. Or as we like to call it in Product Management world, a Roadmap.

## My Somewhat Controversial Opinion

Me personally, and I feel like I'm going against the grain with this a little bit,I tend to believe that maintenance is actually more important than new features.

Generally speaking,at least in terms of direct immediate results,if you're trying to drive new sales, then you need to add new features, right? I mean there has to be some reason to buy the product. It has to have features and you have to add new ones to keep up with the market and your competition.

Maintenance doesn't typically affect your new sales, at least not immediately. Maintenance improves your renewal rate. It determines how many of your new customers turn into recurring customers. If your product is a solid product and there's not a lot of bugs, you're going to have a higher renewal rate. If your product is sloppy and there's lots of bugs or it's very quirky, your renewal rates are going to be subpar.

People might buy it because they bought into your marketing, but then they're going to use it and say, 'I don't like this very much. It's too much of a hassle dealing with the bugs and the workarounds required to make it work.'. Then they won't renew and they'll look for another product.

## The Better Path: Slow Growth with High Retention

Given the choice, if I was the Product Manager for a software with a recurring subscription model, I would much rather grow a product slowly with a high renewal rate, than grow a product quickly with a lower renewal rate.

I know a lot of people hear that and they think, 'Why would anybody want to grow a product slowly?' I mean sure, if you could grow quickly and maintain a high renewal rate, that's obviously ideal. Show me someone who's doing that, though. It's incredibly difficult to pull that off. Especially if you're in a low profit market like WordPress, where teams are small and budgets are smaller.

## The Downward Spiral

Here's the thing: When you have unhappy customers and low renewal rates, guess what else you get?

You get bad reviews. You get a bad reputation. And what does that negatively impacts?

New sales!

If you have a bad reputation, you can add more features till your face turns blue, but you're not going to get those new sales because people don't have confidence in your product. They read reviews that weren't good. They talked to people who told them, 'Yeah, it sounded good at first, but then I encountered this bug and that bug and it just wasn't worth it in the end.'

You also run the risk of growing quickly, then something changes in the market, and now you have to lay off a bunch of people. We see this happen all the time (Zoom during Covid). I don't want to ever be in that position. I like the idea of growing, hiring, providing more jobs, but I don't like the idea of providing a bunch of jobs just to have to take those jobs away.

## The Value of Predictability

I'd much rather have a solid product that I don't have to worry as much about. Sometimes in business bad things happen and there's just nothing you can do about it. Some kind of event happens or the market just changes in ways that you can't control and there's nothing you could have done to predict it or prevent it.

That just happens in business. But sometimes things happen and you could have been more prepared for it, you could have seen it coming, you could have prevented or at least alleviated the problem.

I believe the best way to grow a product is to grow slowly and carefully, while maintaining a very happy customer base with a high renewal rate. I think that will protect you as a business more against outside occurrences that are beyond your control, and I think it will just be better for you in the long run.

Predictability is highly valued in business, though it's not always attainable. If you're making sales, you don't technically need to add new features. When you already have revenue coming in,both new and recurring,and you're maintaining that monthly or annual revenue because your product is solid, your customers are happy, and your renewal rates are high, you have a sustainable business. That's predictability.

## The Feature Treadmill Problem

If you're just pumping out new feature, new feature, new feature, and your marketing is all about 'look at this new feature, look at this new feature,' and your sales are going up and up, you're thinking, 'Yes, things are going so great!'

But at some point, there just aren't any more features you can add. Just be honest with yourself. Eventually, you get to a point where your product has every feature anybody could have dreamed of. Every random-ass feature that some person requested on Twitter,you've added it to your product.

And now you're sitting around going, 'Now what?' And you've made growth predictions and people are expecting this and that, and sales have plateaued. And on top of that, you spent years neglecting your product, creating mountains of technical debt.

## The Technical Debt Monster

Now you have old code that is not meeting best practices anymore. There are new standards and new APIs and things that you just can't take advantage of and integrate with because your code base is so old. Your code has turned into spaghetti code over the years because you focused so much on new features and didn't focus on maintainability, or scalability.

Maybe you made some acquisitions along the way and integrated some third-party things that weren't coded the way you would have coded it had you created that feature yourself. And so you just end up with this sort of Frankenstein product, full of technical debt.

Your developers hate it. Every time you talk about doing something to the plugin, you see a little bit of your developer's soul leave as they know they have to open up that code base one more time because it's a mess.

Then renewal rate starts to drop because the product just gets buggier and buggier over time, you're not increasing your sales anymore because you can't think of any new features to add. And so then you start having to lay people off because you're not making as much money as you were before.

That's not a position I want to be in.

## The Refactoring Conversation

I'm not going to name any companies or products, but I've worked on products where you try to get some stakeholder buy-in on some refactoring and they just shut it down.

You'll have this kind of conversation:

'How long would it take?'

'Well, I talked to the senior developer and he thinks four to six months to completely refactor. And then we'd have basically a brand new squeaky clean plugin, nice and modern, clean code, fairly bug free.'

'No, we can't do that.'

'Why not?'

'Because that'd be six months where we're not adding features.'

'Oh, are people not buying our product anymore?'

'No, they're still buying it.'

'Then why do we have to add new features?'

'Because we want to increase our sales, we want to make more money!'

*sigh*.

## Time Flies and Debt Accumulates

If you grow slowly and carefully, and you put more effort and emphasis into maintaining your software and making sure that the code is good, the code is clean, the code is modern,make sure you're following best practices. If you put an emphasis on that, then you don't have to worry about it.

Because time flies. All of a sudden, you've been working on this product for three years and you didn't realize that you were just slowly adding technical debt to this plugin every sprint, just a little bit more, sprinkling in a little bit more tech debt. And it adds up quick.

Pretty soon you find yourself in a sticky situation, and it's really hard to get out of that situation. The longer you go in that direction, the harder it is to get out of that.

## The TL;DR

So that was a non-brief explanation of what I think is important to the process of creating excellent software. The TL;DR of it all is: I believe a lot of product companies could benefit from focusing more on software maintenance and focusing on scalability and maintainability, and less on MOAR FEATURESSS!!!

Many Product Managers could benefit from shifting that balance. Because it is a balance. You can't completely neglect maintenance or new features and you can't completely neglect new features either.

I'm not saying you've got to stop adding new features. But I do believe that most Product Managers are balancing it a little bit heavy in the wrong direction. And I think they could benefit from tipping the scales a little bit towards maintenance.
